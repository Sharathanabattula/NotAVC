## Headline

LinkedIn allows 220 characters. Three versions, sharpest first.

### Option A — recommended

```
I read the filings that headlines skip | MBA Finance & Derivatives, SR University | Bower School VC/PE | notavc.co
```

*112 characters.* Leads with what you do rather than what you are. The
credentials still land, but second — which is the right order for someone
whose work is the argument.

### Option B — sharper, more opinionated

```
Startup headlines are written by PR firms. I read the filings instead. | MBA Finance & Derivatives | Bower School VC/PE | notavc.co
```

*130 characters.* Higher ceiling and higher risk: it takes a position
before anyone knows who you are. Use this once the archive has enough posts
to back the claim.

### Option C — recruiter-optimised

```
Startup teardowns, one number at a time | MBA Finance & Derivatives, SR University | Bower School LEAD VC/PE | notavc.co
```

*118 characters.* Most searchable — "teardowns", "VC/PE", "MBA Finance"
are all terms a recruiter actually types. Least distinctive.

**Words deliberately absent from all three:** aspiring, passionate,
exploring, enthusiast, journey. Each one signals asking for permission,
and the whole positioning is that you did not wait for any.

## About

LinkedIn allows 2,600 characters. This is ~1,400, which is the range that
gets read.

```
I'm an MBA student at SR University — finance and derivatives — and part of
the Bower School of Entrepreneurship's LEAD VC/PE programme.

This started because a funding announcement made me feel stupid.

Everyone was congratulating a company on a raise. I read the coverage three
times and still couldn't tell what had actually happened. So I found the
filing. Most of the money was debt converting, not new capital. Not a lie
exactly — just a story told the way the people telling it wanted it to land.

That gap between the headline and the arithmetic turned out to be
everywhere. So I started checking.

At NotAVC I take Indian startups apart using public filings, and write down
the exact point where the press release and the numbers stop agreeing. One
company, one number that decides it, and the take I had to correct.

Every post carries the correction — what everyone believed, then what the
filing said. Including the times the person who was wrong was me. Those stay
on the page with a line through them, because a track record you can only
see when it flatters you isn't a track record.

Before this I studied biotech, which is more useful here than it sounds. It
is good training for reading a document where the abstract and the data
don't quite match.

I'm not an investor and I'm not pretending to be. No fund, no track record,
nobody paying me to have an opinion — which is the only reason my read is
worth anything.

If you've ever seen a funding headline and thought "am I stupid, or is this
spin?" — that's who this is for.

Teardowns and term-sheet literacy → notavc.co
Correct my math → sharathanabattula@gmail.com
```

### Why it's built this way

- **Opens with a story, not a claim.** "MBA student passionate about VC" is
  a category. A specific moment where the numbers didn't add up is a
  position, and only you can tell it.
- **The weakness is the credential.** "No fund, nobody paying me" is the
  strongest line on the page — a partner at a fund structurally cannot
  write it.
- **Biotech is reframed, not hidden.** It becomes evidence of how you read
  rather than a detour to explain away.
- **Ends on the reader, not on you.** The last line is the one that makes
  someone follow.

## The harder note on the posts

The ten posts are well written. *"MRR is what you earned. ARR is what you
hope."* and *"Revenue is a fact. Valuation is a feeling."* are genuinely
good lines and the voice is already there.

But across all ten: **no company is named, and no number comes from a
filing.** They are definitions, and definitions don't travel — 66 to 249
impressions is what that ceiling looks like.

The same voice pointed at a real company and a real number is a different
post entirely:

> Accel made 56× on Flipkart and 35× on Swiggy's IPO.
> Their 2014 fund has returned 0.15×.
> Both numbers are true. Only one gets quoted.

Nothing about how you write needs to change. Only what you point it at.
